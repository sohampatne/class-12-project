AIM & OBJECTIVE

AIM OF THE EXPERIMENT

To study the phenomenon of Electromagnetic Induction (Faraday's Law and Lenz's Law) using an insulated copper coil and a moving bar magnet.

SPECIFIC OBJECTIVES

  • To verify that a change in magnetic flux linked with a closed coil induces an electric current in it.
  • To observe the direction of induced current when the North and South poles of a bar magnet are moved towards or away from the coil.
  • To verify Lenz's law by observing the polarity of the induced magnetic field that opposes the motion of the magnet.
  • To demonstrate that the magnitude of the induced EMF depends on the relative speed between the magnet and the coil.
  • To investigate the effect of inserting a high magnetic permeability soft-iron core inside the coil on the magnitude of the induced current.

INTRODUCTION

In the early nineteenth century, electricity and magnetism were treated as completely separate sciences. In the year 1820, Danish physicist Hans Christian Oersted discovered that an electric current flowing through a wire deflects a nearby magnetic compass needle. This revealed for the first time that electric current generates a magnetic field.

Following Oersted's discovery, scientists began asking the reverse question: If an electric current produces a magnetic field, can a magnetic field produce an electric current?

In 1831, English scientist Michael Faraday answered this question conclusively through a series of landmark experiments. Faraday observed that as long as there is relative motion between a magnet and a conducting coil, an electric current is generated in the coil. This process of generating an electric potential difference and electric current by varying magnetic flux is called Electromagnetic Induction (EMI).

This discovery serves as the foundation of modern electrical engineering, making possible the construction of electric generators, dynamos, and power grids worldwide.

THEORY & FORMULATION

1. MAGNETIC FLUX

Magnetic flux represents the total number of magnetic field lines passing perpendicularly through a given surface area. Mathematically:

Φ = B · A = B · A · cos(θ)

Where B is magnetic field strength (Tesla), A is surface area (m²), and θ is the angle between the magnetic field and the normal to the surface.

2. FARADAY'S LAWS OF INDUCTION

First Law: Whenever the magnetic flux linked with a closed circuit changes, an electromotive force (EMF) is induced in the circuit. The induced EMF lasts as long as the change in magnetic flux continues.

Second Law: The magnitude of the induced EMF is directly proportional to the time rate of change of magnetic flux linked with the circuit:

e = - N · (ΔΦ / Δt)

Where N is the number of turns in the coil. The negative sign represents Lenz's Law.

8

LENZ'S LAW

STATEMENT OF LENZ'S LAW

In 1834, German physicist Heinrich Lenz formulated a rule to determine the direction of induced current:

"The polarity of the induced electromotive force is always such that it tends to produce a current which opposes the change in magnetic flux that produces it."

CONSERVATION OF ENERGY

Lenz's law is a direct consequence of the Law of Conservation of Energy:

  • When the North pole of a magnet is pushed towards a coil, the front face develops a North magnetic polarity (anti-clockwise current), creating repulsive magnetic force.
  • To move the magnet closer, mechanical work must be done against this repulsive force.
  • When the North pole is pulled away, the front face develops a South magnetic polarity (clockwise current), creating an attractive force. Work must be done to pull it away.
  • This mechanical work done by the experimenter is converted into electrical energy in the coil. If the current aided the motion instead of opposing it, energy would be created from nothing, violating physics laws.

PROCEDURE

The practical investigation was carried out step-by-step according to the following procedure:

  • Step 1: Clean the copper wire leads using sandpaper to remove insulating varnish, ensuring low contact resistance.
  • Step 2: Connect the terminals of the 500-turn copper solenoid to the center-zero galvanometer using connecting leads. Ensure the pointer is at zero.
  • Step 3: Hold the bar magnet stationary near the coil and record any pointer deflection on the galvanometer scale.
  • Step 4: Push the North pole of the bar magnet rapidly into the center of the coil and observe the direction and magnitude of deflection.
  • Step 5: Hold the magnet stationary inside the coil and note if any steady current flows.
  • Step 6: Withdraw the North pole quickly out of the coil and record the deflection direction.
  • Step 7: Repeat steps 4 to 6 using the South pole facing the coil.
  • Step 8: Move the magnet slowly, then move it very quickly, comparing the scale deflections.
  • Step 9: Insert a soft-iron cylindrical core inside the coil and repeat the rapid insertion of the magnet, noting the enhanced deflection.

ANALYSIS & DISCUSSION

From the observation table, several key physical deductions can be drawn:

  • Necessity of Relative Motion: When the magnet is completely stationary (rows 1 and 3), the magnetic flux linked with the coil is constant (ΔΦ/Δt = 0). Consequently, no EMF is induced and the galvanometer stays at 0.
  • Dependence on Velocity: Comparing row 2 (slow push: 12 div) with row 7 (rapid push: 26 div) demonstrates that induced EMF is directly proportional to the rate of change of magnetic flux (dΦ/dt). Higher speed means the magnetic lines cut through the turns in a shorter time interval.
  • Confirmation of Lenz's Law: Reversing the moving pole (North vs South) or reversing the direction of motion (inward vs outward) consistently reverses the sign of the induced current. This confirms that the induced field acts to oppose the mechanical disturbance.
  • Effect of Soft-Iron Core: Soft iron has a relative permeability (μr) exceeding 1000. It concentrates magnetic lines of force inside the solenoid core, maximizing flux density and driving full-scale needle deflection (+30 div).

RESULTS & APPLICATIONS

EXPERIMENTAL RESULTS

  • The phenomenon of electromagnetic induction was successfully verified using an insulated copper coil and a moving bar magnet.
  • Faraday's laws were verified: Induced EMF appears only during flux change, and its magnitude increases proportionally with relative speed.
  • Lenz's law was experimentally confirmed by the reversal of galvanometer deflection with reversal of motion direction and magnetic polarity.

PRACTICAL APPLICATIONS IN MODERN LIFE

  • Electric AC and DC Generators: Convert rotational mechanical kinetic energy (turbines) into high-voltage electrical energy via electromagnetic induction.
  • Step-Up & Step-Down Transformers: Utilize mutual induction between coils to step voltages up or down for power distribution.
  • Induction Cooktops: High-frequency alternating magnetic fields induce eddy currents in cookware, heating cooking pots directly through Joule heating.
  • Magnetic Braking in High-Speed Trains: Opposing induced eddy currents provide smooth, frictionless deceleration in modern bullet trains.

PRECAUTIONS & ERRORS

PRECAUTIONS TAKEN

  • Clean the ends of connecting copper leads thoroughly with sandpaper to eliminate insulating oxide layers.
  • Ensure the galvanometer pointer is mechanically adjusted exactly to the zero mark before beginning observations.
  • Ensure all electrical terminal connections are tightened firmly to prevent erratic contact resistance.
  • Keep any extraneous magnetic materials, cell phones, and iron objects away from the experimental setup to prevent stray magnetic disturbance.
  • Move the bar magnet smoothly along the central axis of the coil without scraping or damaging the insulated windings.

SOURCES OF ERROR

  • Slight parallax error while observing galvanometer scale readings from an angle.
  • Internal resistance of connecting wires causing slight attenuation in total induced current.
  • Small fluctuations in manual hand movement speed during repeated experimental trials.
