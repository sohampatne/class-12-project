INTRODUCTION

Electrical energy is universally generated, transmitted, and distributed across national grids in the form of alternating current (AC) because alternating voltages can be stepped up or down efficiently using electromagnetic transformers. However, virtually all modern electronic appliances—including computers, mobile phones, televisions, integrated circuits, and battery charging systems—strictly require smooth, stable direct current (DC) for their internal operations.

The electrical process of converting bidirectional alternating current into unidirectional direct current is known as rectification, and the circuit designed to accomplish this function is called a rectifier. The basic building block of any electronic rectifier is the semiconductor P-N junction diode, which possesses the unique property of allowing electrical current to pass easily in one direction (forward bias) while presenting very high resistance in the opposite direction (reverse bias).

While a simple half-wave rectifier uses only one diode and utilizes merely one half-cycle of the AC input (resulting in a low theoretical efficiency of 40.6% and large pulsating ripples), a full-wave bridge rectifier utilizes both positive and negative half-cycles of the AC wave. By configuring four interconnected diodes in a closed bridge network, it delivers a significantly higher theoretical efficiency of 81.2% and provides a far smoother DC output when paired with a filter capacitor.

AIM & PRINCIPLE

AIM OF THE EXPERIMENT:

To construct a working model of a Full-Wave Bridge Rectifier with a Capacitor Filter using four P-N junction diodes (1N4007), a step-down transformer, and an electrolytic capacitor, and to observe the conversion of alternating voltage (AC) into smooth direct voltage (DC).

PRINCIPLE OF OPERATION:

The operation of a full-wave bridge rectifier is based on two fundamental scientific principles:

  • Unidirectional Conductivity of Diodes: A semiconductor P-N junction diode conducts electrical current only when forward-biased (anode at higher potential than cathode). Under reverse bias, the depletion layer widens and prevents current flow.
  • Full-Cycle Bridge Conduction: Four diodes ($D_1, D_2, D_3, D_4$) are arranged in a bridge loop. In the positive half-cycle, diodes $D_1$ and $D_4$ conduct. In the negative half-cycle, diodes $D_2$ and $D_3$ conduct. In both cycles, current traverses the external load resistor ($R_L$) in the exact same direction, yielding full-wave DC.
  • Capacitive Energy Storage: A parallel capacitor charges to peak voltage during conduction and slowly discharges through the load during the non-conducting valley, smoothing out pulsating ripples into steady DC.

THEORY & DERIVATIONS

Let the alternating voltage input across the secondary winding of the step-down transformer be represented by the sinusoidal equation:

V_i = V_m sin(ωt)

Where $V_m$ represents the peak secondary voltage and $\omega = 2\pi f$ is the angular frequency (at $f = 50\, ext{Hz}$).

1. Average / DC Output Voltage ($V_{dc}$):

Since both half-cycles are rectified, the average direct voltage is twice that of a half-wave rectifier:

V_{dc} = (2 · V_m) / π ≈ 0.636 · V_m

2. Rectification Efficiency (η):

Efficiency is the ratio of DC output power to AC input power:

η = P_{dc} / P_{ac} = (8 / π²) · [R_L / (R_L + 2r_f)] ≈ 81.2%

This demonstrates that full-wave bridge rectification achieves exactly twice the maximum theoretical efficiency of a half-wave rectifier (40.6%).

WORKING OF BRIDGE CIRCUIT

The four diodes ($D_1, D_2, D_3, D_4$) are interconnected in a closed loop forming a bridge network. The AC secondary winding is connected to opposite diagonal junctions, while the load resistor $R_L$ is connected across the remaining two junctions.

A. During Positive Half-Cycle of AC Input:

Terminal A of the transformer becomes positive with respect to Terminal B. Consequently:

  • Diodes $D_1$ and $D_4$ become forward-biased and conduct electricity.
  • Diodes $D_2$ and $D_3$ become reverse-biased and block current flow.
  • Current flows from Terminal A $ ightarrow D_1 ightarrow$ Load Resistor $R_L$ (from top to bottom) $ ightarrow D_4 ightarrow$ Terminal B.

B. During Negative Half-Cycle of AC Input:

Terminal B becomes positive with respect to Terminal A:

  • Diodes $D_2$ and $D_3$ become forward-biased and conduct electricity.
  • Diodes $D_1$ and $D_4$ become reverse-biased and block current flow.
  • Current flows from Terminal B $ ightarrow D_2 ightarrow$ Load Resistor $R_L$ (from top to bottom) $ ightarrow D_3 ightarrow$ Terminal A.

Crucial Observation: Current flows through the load resistor $R_L$ in the identical direction during both half-cycles, producing continuous DC!

ROLE OF FILTER CAPACITOR

The rectified output obtained from the bridge circuit is pulsating DC consisting of a steady DC component superimposed with unwanted alternating harmonics known as ripples. Directly feeding pulsating DC to sensitive electronics causes operational malfunction and humming noise.

To eliminate ripples, an electrolytic capacitor of high capacitance ($C = 1000\,\mu ext{F}$) is placed in parallel across the load resistor $R_L$. The capacitor functions as a dynamic energy reservoir:

  • Charging Phase: As the rectified voltage rises towards its peak value $V_m$, the capacitor charges rapidly through the low forward resistance of the conducting diodes, reaching peak voltage $V_m$.
  • Discharging Phase: When the rectified voltage begins to fall below $V_m$, the diodes become reverse-biased. The capacitor discharges stored charge slowly through the load resistor $R_L$. Because the time constant ($ au = R_L \cdot C$) is designed to be much larger than the half-period of the AC wave ($T/2 = 10\, ext{ms}$), voltage across $R_L$ drops only slightly before the next peak charges it again.

Formula for Ripple Factor (r):

r = V_{ac} / V_{dc} = 1 / (4√3 · f · C · R_L)

Higher capacitance $C$ substantially minimizes the ripple factor, providing smooth, battery-like DC voltage.

EXPERIMENTAL PROCEDURE

The experimental verification was conducted systematically using the following practical steps:

  • Step 1 - Component Verification: Each 1N4007 diode was tested using a digital multimeter in diode testing mode to verify polarity (silver band indicates cathode) and check for forward voltage drop (~0.7V).
  • Step 2 - Bridge Network Assembly: Four diodes were interconnected on the circuit board in a closed bridge configuration, taking strict care that cathodes of $D_1, D_2$ meet at the positive DC terminal and anodes of $D_3, D_4$ meet at the negative DC ground.
  • Step 3 - Transformer Secondary Connection: The secondary leads of the 9V step-down transformer were soldered to the remaining two opposite diagonal AC junctions of the bridge.
  • Step 4 - Filter and Load Connection: The $1000\,\mu ext{F}$ electrolytic capacitor, $1\, ext{k}\Omega$ load resistor, and green output LED were connected in parallel across the DC output terminals.
  • Step 5 - Testing & Measurements: The transformer primary was connected to 230V AC mains. Input AC voltage and output DC voltages (both without and with filter capacitor) were measured using a digital voltmeter.

ADVANTAGES & APPLICATIONS

KEY ADVANTAGES OF BRIDGE RECTIFIER:

  • High Rectification Efficiency: Delivers maximum theoretical efficiency of 81.2%, exactly twice that of a half-wave rectifier.
  • No Center-Tapped Transformer Required: Works with standard two-lead step-down transformers, reducing bulk, weight, and manufacturing cost compared to center-tap designs.
  • Low Peak Inverse Voltage (PIV): The PIV rating required for each diode is only $V_m$ (half of the $2V_m$ rating needed in center-tapped full-wave rectifiers), allowing cheaper diode selection.

IMPORTANT REAL-WORLD APPLICATIONS:

  • Mobile Phone & Laptop Chargers: Forms the front-end AC to DC conversion stage in all Switched Mode Power Supplies (SMPS).
  • Battery Charging Systems: Automobile alternators convert AC into DC using bridge rectifiers to charge lead-acid vehicle batteries.
  • Household Consumer Electronics: Internal DC supply for televisions, audio amplifiers, microcontrollers, and LED lighting drivers.
